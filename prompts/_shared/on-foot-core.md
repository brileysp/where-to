## On-foot family rules

Hiking & Trekking, Scenic Landscapes, Landscape Photography, Camping & Backcountry and Trail Running cover much of the same ground, and the same place is often written for several of them. Each has its own scope: what counts toward its score and what it is about. Scope never stops you from mentioning something that another interest also covers. A Lofoten scenic text can say the views are best enjoyed on the hikes; a hiking text can name a viewpoint anyone can drive to. The same fact, sentence or whole blurb may appear in two interests when it fits both.

| Interest | It is about | Does not count toward its score |
|---|---|---|
| Hiking & Trekking | Walking trails, from short day hikes to multi-day treks: the routes, when they are open, how hard they are, what it takes to walk them, and the scenery and views the walking earns | Views anyone can reach without walking, sleeping outside, running, climbing with ropes |
| Scenic Landscapes | The landscape as a sight, for anyone: the viewpoints, drives, short walks and boat trips that show it, and the landscape as a whole | Trail planning and difficulty, photography technique |
| Landscape Photography | When, where and in what light the landscape photographs best | Trail planning |
| Camping & Backcountry | Spending nights outside, self-supported: where, permits, season, wildlife rules | Day hikes, the view |
| Trail Running | Running trails: the networks, the races, the terrain and the heat or altitude | Walking routes |

**Scenery counts.** For the best walks the landscape is the reason to walk, so scenery is a main factor in a Hiking score and part of its text, especially scenery and views that can only be reached on foot (a ridge above the lakes, a glacier valley, a summit panorama). The same goes for scenery earned by running or by sleeping out in Trail Running and Camping & Backcountry.

**What counts as on foot, and how far.** A trail or route a visitor can walk within a day trip of the place. For a city or small place that means about an hour or so away; an outstanding hike may be up to about two hours each way, and the text says the drive when it is more than an hour. Walking tours of a city and scenic drives do not count here. A city park, a promenade or a short nature walk is the lowest rung of hiking, not nothing. A hike that clearly belongs to another place in the catalog is left to that place (when you are given the list of other places, it is under `otherPlacesInCatalog`).

**Where hiking stops.** A summit reached on foot with no technical gear counts as hiking (Kilimanjaro, a trekking-peak walk-up). One that needs a rope, crampons and an ice axe on its main route belongs to Mountaineering.

**One scale for difficulty, so the family uses the same words.** These bands are for your own judgment; the text does not use the band names as labels; it says in plain words what a route is like and what makes it hard.
- **Easy:** under 3 miles, graded or paved, little climbing.
- **Middle band:** a day hike of 3 to 10 miles, up to about 2,000 ft of climbing.
- **Strenuous:** reserved for a day that would test even a serious hiker: more than 12 miles with real climbing, more than about 3,500 ft of climbing, high altitude, or serious scrambling and exposure. Short steep descents and ordinary rough ground are not strenuous.
- **Trek:** a route of two or more days.
- **Expedition trek:** a trek that goes above about 14,000 ft or is days from help.

These thresholds are for your own judgment. Do not repeat them as numbers in the text: a day hike is named by its level word, and a length is given only for a multi-day route or a day of 15 miles or more.

**Units.** Distances in miles, elevations and climbing in feet, walking time in hours (or days for a trek), temperatures in Fahrenheit. American spelling.

**Snow.** Groomed winter walking trails and snowshoe routes count as walking; a trail that needs snowshoes here and there still counts, and the month says so. A place that has them is not dead in winter. A month when the only way through is on cross-country skis or ski touring, or when nothing at all is walkable, is not walking: it scores 0.0 for Hiking.

**What drives a walking season.** Snow and the date a pass clears, monsoon or heavy rain, heat (start before dawn), daylight, the dates huts and transport operate, insects, wildfire smoke, permit windows. Name the driver in the month where it matters, and give the elevation where the season depends on it.

**Named things are real.** Name a trail, pass, hut or viewpoint only when you are sure it exists and sure of what it is. A route is named the way hikers say it in English. A multi-day route gets its length and days; a day hike gets its level word and no length, unless the day is 15 miles or more.

**Safety, said plainly.** Altitude sickness, exposure, river crossings, avalanche slopes, heat, bears and other wildlife, and closures are stated where they are real, in a few words, and not elsewhere. Say when a guide or a permit is required. Do not frighten with hazards that are not an issue at that place.

**No operators.** No tour companies, guides, outfitters or hotels by name. A hut, refuge or tea house is named only when the route is built around it.

**Keep out** politics, security and environmental crises (see the voice rules).
