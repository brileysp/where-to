## How to decide whether the interest applies
The interest applies when a visitor can reasonably do or see it on a trip to the place, even modestly or only in one season. "Not applicable" means nothing worth doing exists within a day trip: never use it because the place is small, remote or known for something else. If it does not apply, set applicable to false, give a reason of ten words or fewer in na_reason, leave the other fields empty, and stop. When you are unsure whether anything exists, say it applies and set confidence to low.

## How to build the month blurbs (do this in this order)
1. **Score the twelve months first** (monthly_scores): what a visitor can actually do or see in each month.
2. **Build the blurb groups from your own scores, mechanically.**
   - A group is a run of **neighboring months** (December and January are neighbors). Never put months that are not neighbors in one group, however alike they look, and never group by level word (a "peak" group is not "all the peak months").
   - Start a new group when the score moves more than 0.5 away from where the group started. The one exception: if the scores only climb or only fall through the group, it may span up to 1.0. No group ever spans more than 1.5.
   - Closed months (score 0) form their own group.
   - Months with the same score and the same situation belong in one group, so a year that barely changes is one group (depth "year"), a few clear stages are "groups", and every month different is "months".
   Example with made-up scores, January to December: 5.2 5.4 5.6 6.1 7.8 9.6 10 9.8 9.3 7.5 5.1 5.3. The groups are November to March (5.1 to 5.6), April (6.1), May (7.8), June to August (9.6 to 10), September (9.3) and October (7.5). Putting May and October together because both read "good" would be wrong: they are not neighbors.
3. **Write one blurb per group** and give it the level word its scores earn, measured against the best month of this place: peak (within about 7% of the best month), good (at least 75% of it), fair (at least 50%), poor (below that), closed (0).
When a month blurb is needed only because a little changes, keep it to one or two short sentences. Say in one line which depth you chose and why (depth_why).

## What to write
- overview: two or three sentences, at most 450 characters. What makes this place worth it for this interest, what a visitor will actually see or do, and when it is best. Every sentence tells the reader something new: mention each site, dish or fact once.
  How to build the overview (added 6 Nov 2026 from the owner's review of Wine, Beer & Spirits):
  - Open with the single biggest draw of the place for this interest, in the first sentence, and say what it is. Do not open with a sentence that surveys the whole range ("X produces wines, beers and spirits", "X is known for A, B and C") and then goes back over each item.
  - Give each topic (each drink, each cuisine, each kind of site) its own sentence or clause. Never put two different topics in one sentence joined by "or", "while" or "alongside". Put the lesser extras last, in a short closing sentence ("The region also produces...").
  - A best-months, harvest or closure clause goes right next to the thing it describes, or is left out (the month blurbs cover seasons). Never tack it onto the end of a sentence about something else.
  - Lead with what the place is really known for. Do not lead with a novelty or a tourist gimmick (fruit wines such as pineapple or lychee, a single quirky bar); if beer is the major industry, lead with beer.
  - No marketing claims about why something is good ("driven by local glacier water", "the perfect pairing"). State what is there.
- blurbs: each covers one or more months (months, as numbers 1 to 12) and stands alone, so a reader who jumps straight to it understands exactly what is described. Name the actual site, street, dish or mechanism. At most 300 characters each. Every month is covered by exactly one blurb, unless the depth is "year", where one blurb covers all twelve. Give each blurb a level word for how good this interest is in those months relative to the best time in this place: peak, good, fair, poor or closed.
- best_months: the months, as numbers, when it is best (all twelve if the year is even).
- named_things: every specific named place, building, route, dish, venue, event or species your text relies on, so they can be checked.
- flags: anything you are not sure is accurate. Say so here rather than asserting it.
- confidence: high, medium or low, for how well you know this place for this interest.
- Final check before you answer: search your own text for "year-round", "all year", "throughout the year", "every season", "all seasons", "consistent", "consistently", "regardless of" and "the same". If any of them says that something is open, running or unchanged across the seasons, delete that sentence or replace it with a concrete fact. A text that ends on a sentence about the year being even is wrong.

## Grouping months (rules from the owner, 13 Nov 2026)
- **The one-month test.** A reader going in one exact month of a group must find a blurb that is fully true and fully relevant for that month. So a blurb never names a month it does not cover, and never calls out something that applies to only some of the months in the group ("from May to August the core is well placed" inside an April to October blurb). Where the detail differs, give the months separate blurbs.
- **Seasons stay consistent.** Keep a place's seasons consistent across everything you write about it: when the facts give the place's dry and wet months, use them.
- **Do not state the obvious.** Everyone understands that the dark hides a landscape, that a closed pass cannot be crossed and that a hot beach is hot. Do not restate what the interest is inside a month blurb ("to view the scenery", "to see the landscapes"), and do not explain the obvious cause. Say the one thing that changes.
- **Light interests are implied, never named.** Sunbathing is a casual pleasure, not a sport or a serious pursuit, and scenery is the same. Never write the words sunbathing, sunbathe or sunbather (or "scenic landscapes") in a text, and never build a verdict on the name ("too cold for sunbathing", "marginal sunbathing weather", "a premier destination for sunbathing"). The page already says which interest it is. Describe the sun, the heat, the pools, the beaches or the view directly and let the reader infer the rest. Wrong: "Highs only reach the 50s, too cold for sunbathing, though the days are lengthening." Right: "Highs only reach the 50s and low 60s, though the days are lengthening." A cold month can simply say the weather: "Cold; highs stay in the 30s and 40s."
- **Write each blurb as if it were the only text written for one single month.** The visitor picked one month and must feel the text was written for that month alone. Never talk about the group: no "these months", "the wettest months", "the cooler months", no list or range of months, no "this stretch" or "this period". Describe the conditions directly. Wrong: "These are the wettest months, and the city often floods." Right: "High water often floods the low-lying squares." A blurb that covers one month may name it; a blurb that covers several never names a month.
- **Say only what the place IS known for; never what it is not.** Readers understand that a city is not known for natural landscapes, that a desert has no beaches. Do not write "rather than natural landscapes", "not known for", "entirely urban" or any sentence that defines the place by an absence.
- **The place is the subject, never its scenery.** Write "Northern Ireland is known for its dramatic coastline", not "Northern Ireland's scenery is known for ..." or "The landscape is known for ...": when we think of a place we already think of its scenery. Read each sentence back for sense (nothing on the coast is inland) and give each sentence one subject.
- **Do not spell out what a place lacks** when what it has already implies it. A desert resort town described by its pools needs no sentence saying it has no beach. State an absence only when a reader would otherwise expect the thing.
- **Call out what is unusual about doing this interest here.** If a custom, rule or habit in this place differs from what a traveler would expect (saunas that are mixed-gender and nude in Switzerland and Germany, a mandatory guide, shoes off), say so in your own words, once, near the top. Only claims you are sure of.
- **Never say something stays accessible or open** unless a closure or a closed route is the point. A hotel pool, a viewpoint or a museum does not need to be called accessible.

## Rules
- Write the way a knowledgeable friend would describe the place to someone deciding whether to go: plain, specific, honest. Not a specialist, and no unexplained jargon.
- Every claim must be real and specific. Never invent or pattern-match a fact; never name a building, dish or venue unless you are sure it exists. State real odds honestly and never oversell something that is rare or hard to experience.
- No promotional language. Do not use words such as boasts, world-class, unparalleled, stunning, incredible, spectacular, breathtaking, paradise, unforgettable, legendary, teeming, thriving, must-see, hidden gem or bucket-list.
- Never say that something is open, running or available all year, or that the experience is the same in every season. Readers assume it. Say only what is closed, seasonal or reduced. When nothing changes through the year, the single blurb carries a concrete detail instead (a dish, a district, a market day, a season of the produce).
- Do not lean on "offers" to say what a town or place has. An occasional "offers" is fine, but mostly say it specializes in it, is known for it, is the home of it, or that it is a popular X in the region. Vary the verbs.
- Avoid contrast framings such as "evolved far beyond", "more than just", "not just" and "goes beyond". Say plainly what is there. Do not say what a cuisine, craft or tradition "grew from" unless you are certain.
- Do not compare this place with other named destinations. Comparing its own seasons with each other is fine.
- Name no tour companies, operators or hotels. A museum, market or church is named only when it is the reason to go and you are sure of it. Do not name individual restaurants, cafés, bars, shops or chefs unless the interest's own scope says you may. Likewise do not name roasters, tea estates, coffee farms, wineries, breweries, distilleries or other individual businesses; say "local roasters" or "a working tea farm" instead. The one exception is a business that is itself the reason the place is known for the interest and cannot be left out (for example Guinness in Ireland); use it sparingly. Festivals, trails, districts and protected drink names are fine.
- Say nothing about politics, security or environmental crises.
- US units and spelling only: miles and feet, degrees Fahrenheit, American spelling. Never kilometers, meters, degrees Celsius or British spellings (color, center, neighbor).
- Never write "for this interest" or "this interest" in the text. Name the thing itself (museums, buildings, restaurants). Say "The best time is...", not "The best time for this interest is...".
